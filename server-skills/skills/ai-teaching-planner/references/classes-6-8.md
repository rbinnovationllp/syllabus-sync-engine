# Classes 6–8: Understanding and Building AI Systems

ELI level: Class 6 = ELI11, Class 7 = ELI12, Class 8 = ELI13. Ages ~11–14.

## Topic list (sequence roughly in this order)

1. AI, machine learning, automation, algorithms, and robotics — how they relate and differ
2. How an AI system works — input → data → model/rules → output → human feedback
3. Computational thinking for AI — decomposition, patterns, abstraction, algorithms, and logical reasoning
4. Data collection — where AI data comes from
5. Data quality — incomplete, inaccurate, outdated, duplicated, or unbalanced data
6. Data representation and visualisation — tables, charts, categories, text, images, sound, and numbers
7. Training data and testing data — why AI must be tested on examples it did not simply memorise
8. Features and labels — what information an AI system uses to learn
9. Classification and prediction
10. Rule-based systems versus learning-based systems

12. Model training, testing, errors, and improvement — conceptual level without advanced mathematics
13. Accuracy and simple model evaluation — when "correct most of the time" may still not be good enough

15. Natural language processing — how AI works with human language

18. Generative AI — text, images, audio, video, and code
19. How generative AI differs from search engines and traditional software
20. Large language models — a conceptual introduction without transformer mathematics
21. Tokens, patterns, prediction, and context — intuitive explanation of how language models generate responses
22. Prompting — task, context, constraints, examples, and checking the output
23. AI hallucinations — why fluent/confident answers may still be wrong
24. Verification and AI-assisted research — checking claims against reliable sources
25. Embeddings and semantic similarity — optional intuitive introduction for Class 8
26. AI agents and tool use — introductory concept of AI systems that can plan steps and use approved tools

28. Robotics, sensors, perception, and intelligent control
29. Edge AI and cloud AI — where AI processing can happen and why this matters
30. Privacy and personal data

32. Bias and fairness — how datasets and design choices can affect different people differently
33. Explainability and transparency — asking why an AI system produced a result
34. Human oversight and accountability — people remain responsible for important decisions
35. Deepfakes, synthetic media, misinformation, and impersonation
36. Copyright, attribution, originality, and responsible use of AI-generated content
37. AI and society — effects on education, jobs, creativity, accessibility, and everyday life

## Progression within this band

### Class 6 — Understand AI Systems

Primary goal: move from recognising AI applications to understanding the components of an AI system.

Emphasise:
- AI vs automation vs machine learning
- Computational thinking
- Input/output
- Data collection
- Data quality
- Training/testing
- Features and labels
- Classification
- Rule-based vs learning-based systems
- Computer vision
- Speech/language applications
- Privacy and responsible use

Students should begin asking:

"What information does this system use?"

"How does it produce its result?"

"How could it be wrong?"

### Class 7 — Apply and Evaluate AI

Primary goal: use and evaluate simple AI systems.

Emphasise:
- Machine-learning workflow
- Model training/testing
- Simple evaluation
- Computer vision
- NLP
- Speech recognition
- Recommendation systems
- Generative AI
- Prompting
- Hallucinations
- Verification
- Bias and fairness
- Deepfakes
- No-code AI projects

Students should increasingly:

train → test → identify errors → improve → test again.

### Class 8 — Build and Question AI Systems

Primary goal: transition toward creating simple AI systems and critically evaluating modern AI.

Emphasise:
- LLM concepts
- Context and prompting
- Semantic similarity/embeddings at an intuitive level
- AI agents and tool use
- Automation workflows
- Robotics and sensors
- Edge vs cloud AI
- Explainability
- Cybersecurity
- Human oversight
- Bharatiya-language AI
- AI system design
- Introductory Python
- AI innovation
- Community problem solving
- Capstone project

By the end of Class 8, students should progress from:

"What can AI do?"

toward:

"How could we design, test, improve, and responsibly use an AI system to address this problem?"

## Calibration for this band

- **Language**: use correct AI vocabulary, but explain every technical term through concrete examples before formalising it.
- **Depth**: students should understand the logic of an AI system without university-level mathematics.
- **Conceptual model**: repeatedly use the structure:

  Problem → Data/Input → AI Method → Output → Evaluation → Human Decision → Improvement

- **Data**: students should work with small, safe datasets and learn collection, cleaning, labelling, representation, quality, imbalance, and testing.
- **Machine learning**: students should understand that learning-based systems find patterns from examples rather than receiving every possible rule explicitly.
- **Evaluation**: do not teach that accuracy alone proves a model is good. Use simple examples showing why errors affecting different groups or important decisions matter.
- **Generative AI**: students may use teacher-approved generative AI for guided learning, creation, comparison, and experimentation, but every activity should include verification or reflection.
- **LLMs**: explain language models through patterns, tokens, context, prediction, and examples. Do not introduce transformer mathematics at this band.
- **Prompting**: progress beyond "ask a good question." Teach task + context + constraints + examples + output format + verification.
- **Agents**: introduce agents only as systems that can pursue a goal through multiple steps and may use approved tools. Do not imply autonomous AI is human-like.
- **Coding**: Class 6 may remain primarily no-code/block-based. Introduce Python progressively in Classes 7–8, with Class 8 receiving the strongest exposure.
- **Python**: teach enough programming to manipulate simple data and understand later AI projects. Do not turn the AI curriculum into a general Python course.
- **Math**: use age-appropriate percentages, averages, coordinates, tables, charts, simple probability intuition, and comparison of model results. Avoid calculus, matrix algebra, or formal optimisation.
- **Projects**: projects should increasingly follow problem → design → build → test → improve → explain.
- **Assessment**: combine conceptual questions, practical activities, model evaluation, short reflections, demonstrations, and projects rather than relying primarily on written examinations.
- **Responsible AI**: privacy, fairness, safety, verification, accountability, and human oversight should appear throughout the curriculum rather than in one isolated ethics chapter.
- **Human-centred principle**: students must understand that AI should support human capability and decision-making rather than automatically replace human judgement.
- **Bharat context**: regularly use examples involving Indian languages, agriculture, accessibility, education, healthcare, climate, MSMEs, public services, and local communities.
- **Inclusivity**: examine whether an AI system works equally well across languages, accents, genders, skin tones, abilities, regions, and social contexts where age appropriate.
- **Tool independence**: teach concepts transferable across AI systems rather than training students to operate only one commercial product.
- **Teacher oversight**: student use of external generative-AI tools must follow school policy, age requirements, privacy requirements, and teacher supervision.
- **Evidence mindset**: students should increasingly be expected to justify statements such as "this model works well" using test results rather than impressions.

## Expected learning outcomes by the end of Class 8

A student should be able to:

1. Explain the differences and relationships between AI, machine learning, automation, algorithms, and robotics.
2. Describe a simple AI system using input, data, model/rules, output, evaluation, and human feedback.
3. Apply decomposition, pattern recognition, abstraction, and algorithms to a problem.
4. Explain why data quality matters.
5. Collect, clean, label, organise, and visualise a small safe dataset.
6. Explain training data and testing data.
7. Identify simple features and labels.
8. Build/train a simple classification system using an age-appropriate tool.
9. Test the system on new examples.
10. Measure and discuss its errors using simple evaluation measures.
11. Explain why high overall accuracy does not automatically mean an AI system is fair or suitable.
12. Explain basic applications of computer vision, NLP, speech recognition, recommendation systems, and robotics.
13. Explain the basic purpose of generative AI.
14. Describe, conceptually, how an LLM uses patterns and context to generate language.
15. Create progressively better prompts using task, context, constraints, examples, and output requirements.
16. Recognise hallucinations and verify important AI-generated claims.
17. Explain at an introductory level how AI-generated media can contribute to misinformation and deepfakes.
18. Explain why personal or sensitive information should not be unnecessarily given to AI systems.
19. Identify examples of bias and fairness problems.
20. Explain why humans should remain accountable for important AI-assisted decisions.
21. Describe the basic idea of an AI agent and an automation workflow.
22. Explain the difference between cloud-based and device/edge AI at an introductory level.
23. Create a small no-code/block-based AI project.
24. Write and understand introductory Python programs appropriate to this band.
25. Use Python to perform a small data/AI-related experiment where resources permit.
26. Identify meaningful applications of AI in Bharat.
27. Explain why multilingual AI is particularly important in India.
28. Recognise examples of Indian AI initiatives such as BHASHINI and IndiaAI at an age-appropriate level.
29. Decide whether AI is actually appropriate for a proposed problem.
30. Design an AI solution while considering users, data, testing, privacy, fairness, safety, and human oversight.
31. Work responsibly with AI as a collaborator without outsourcing their own reasoning.
32. Complete and present an AI capstone project that includes both technical results and reflection on limitations and responsible use.

## Sample analogies that work at this level

- Training data is like practice material; testing data is like a new examination question the system has not already practised.
- Features are clues the system can use to distinguish one thing from another.
- A label is like the answer written on a training example.
- A rule-based system is like following a fixed rulebook; a learning-based system discovers useful patterns from examples.
- Model evaluation is like checking a student's answers rather than assuming they learned something simply because they studied.
- A recommendation system is like a shopkeeper making suggestions from previous choices, except the computer can analyse patterns across enormous numbers of interactions.
- An LLM can be introduced as an extremely sophisticated pattern-based language generator: it uses context to predict useful continuations, but fluent language does not guarantee factual truth.
- Context is like giving someone the previous pages of a story before asking what should come next.
- A hallucination is like confidently giving an answer that sounds believable even though the supporting facts are missing or wrong.
- An AI agent is like giving a digital helper a goal, a set of permitted tools, and rules about what it may do — but a person should still decide its authority and check important actions.
- Edge AI is like doing the work inside the device; cloud AI is like sending the task to a powerful computer elsewhere and receiving the result.
- Bias in data is like preparing for a cricket match by practising only one kind of bowling and then assuming you are equally prepared for every bowler.

## Bharat-focused learning strand

Bharat-focused AI should be woven through Classes 6–8 rather than taught only as one isolated lesson.

Students should progressively explore:

### Bharatiya Language AI

Explore:

- Speech recognition
- Text-to-speech
- Translation
- Transliteration
- OCR
- Code-mixed language
- Different accents and dialects
- Low-resource languages

Discuss why building AI for India's linguistic diversity can be more difficult than designing only for one widely represented language.

### BHASHINI

Introduce BHASHINI as a real Indian example of language technology.

Students do not need to memorise administrative details.

Instead investigate questions such as:

"How could speech technology help two Indians who speak different languages communicate?"

"Why might an AI system understand one accent better than another?"

"How can technology improve digital accessibility?"

### IndiaAI and India's AI Ecosystem

By Class 8, introduce IndiaAI at an age-appropriate conceptual level.

Discuss why Bharat needs:

- AI skills
- Computing infrastructure
- High-quality Indian datasets
- Indigenous AI development
- Safe and trusted AI
- AI startups
- AI research
- AI for public benefit

Do not turn this into memorisation of government schemes.

### AI for Bharat Challenges

Projects may address problems such as:

- Crop support
- Water conservation
- Waste segregation
- Local-language communication
- Accessibility for persons with disabilities
- School learning
- Traffic
- Air quality
- Community health awareness
- Disaster preparedness
- Supporting small businesses
- Preserving Bharatiya languages and cultural knowledge responsibly

Students should be encouraged to ask:

"Who benefits?"

"Who could be excluded?"

"What data would we need?"

"Should we use AI at all?"

"What happens if the AI is wrong?"

"Who should check the result?"

## Capstone expectations

By Class 8, every substantial capstone should include:

1. Problem statement
2. Intended users
3. Why the problem matters
4. Whether AI is actually needed
5. Data/input required
6. Privacy considerations
7. Proposed AI approach
8. Prototype or demonstration
9. Testing
10. Errors discovered
11. Improvement attempted
12. Bias/fairness consideration
13. Human oversight
14. Limitations
15. Final presentation/reflection

A visually impressive demo is not enough.

Students should be rewarded for identifying limitations and failures honestly.

## Safety rules

Students should understand:

- Never casually provide passwords, OTPs, financial details, private photographs, health information, precise location, or other sensitive information to AI systems.
- Do not upload another person's personal information without appropriate permission.
- Treat AI-generated factual claims as claims to verify, not guaranteed facts.
- Never use AI-generated content to impersonate, bully, threaten, defraud, or embarrass another person.
- Deepfake voices/images/videos can cause real harm.
- Do not follow AI-generated instructions involving dangerous activities.
- AI-generated code should not automatically be run without understanding and appropriate supervision.
- Human approval should remain necessary before an AI agent performs consequential actions.
- School policies and applicable age/privacy requirements take priority over convenience.

## Avoid

- Calculus, matrix algebra, gradient descent derivations, or advanced ML mathematics.
- Detailed transformer architecture.
- Training large neural networks.
- Complex API/backend development.
- Presenting LLMs as if they "know everything."
- Equating AI competence with chatbot competence.
- Teaching prompt tricks without verification and critical thinking.
- Allowing students to use AI as a substitute for learning to write, calculate, reason, research, or create.
- Unsupervised use of unsuitable public AI platforms.
- Uploading sensitive student data to public AI systems.
- Treating accuracy as the only measure of model quality.
- Presenting every automation as AI.
- Assuming every problem requires AI.
- Brand-dependent curriculum that will become obsolete when individual products change.
- Memorisation of Indian programmes or company/model names instead of understanding their purpose.

## Calibration for this band
- **Language**: proper technical terms used consistently, each still defined on first use; students should leave able to use the vocabulary correctly.
- **Depth**: concepts now connect into a system view — e.g., data → training → model → prediction as one pipeline, not isolated facts.
- **Examples**: real apps students use (spam filters, photo tagging, streaming recommendations), plus Indian-context cases (UPI fraud detection, regional-language voice assistants, agri/weather apps).
- **Lesson length**: 40–60 minutes; can sustain multi-step demonstrations and small-group work.
- **Activities**: hands-on classification exercises (sort real or simulated data), building a simple rule-based chatbot, feature/label identification exercises, bias-spotting exercises with real (age-appropriate) examples.
- **Math**: basic statistics — averages, simple percentages, reading a confusion-matrix-lite (correct vs. incorrect counts), simple graphs. No calculus/linear algebra.
- **Coding**: no-code and block-based tools remain central; introduce simple Python (e.g., using a pre-built dataset with a few lines of code, or a beginner-friendly ML tool like Teachable Machine) as an option, not a requirement.
- **Projects**: single/double-period projects growing toward a small multi-session project by Class 8 — e.g., train a simple image classifier with a no-code tool, build a rule-based chatbot, analyze bias in a small sample dataset.
- **Assessment**: MCQ, short/long answer, scenario-based questions requiring reasoning ("why did this classifier get it wrong?"), practical tasks, reflection questions on ethics.
- **Responsible AI**: case-study based — real (simplified) examples of bias, misinformation, deepfakes; students discuss and identify problems, not just receive rules.

## Sample analogies that work at this level
- Training data is like a textbook the machine studies — if the textbook is wrong or incomplete, so is the machine's understanding.
- A neural-network-lite intuition: like a student who gets better at a task by seeing many worked examples and adjusting their guesses.
- Bias in AI is like a librarian who only ever recommends books from one shelf because that's all they were shown.

Avoid: full neural network math, backpropagation, production-grade coding, unqualified claims that AI "understands" things.
