## Calibration for this band

- **Language**: use correct technical terminology. Students should progressively become comfortable with terms such as dataset, feature, label, classification, regression, clustering, model, training, validation, inference, evaluation, precision, recall, neural network, token, embedding, LLM, RAG, API, and agent.
- **Depth**: concepts should be technically meaningful without becoming an undergraduate computer-science course.
- **Math**: use percentages, averages, graphs, coordinates, basic algebra, probability intuition, distance/similarity, and simple metric calculations. Explain the mathematics behind accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 at an age-appropriate level.
- **Statistics**: introduce distributions, sampling, averages, variation, correlation versus causation, and uncertainty conceptually where they support AI understanding.
- **Machine learning**: students should actually train, test, compare, and improve small models rather than only memorising algorithm definitions.
- **Data**: students should work with real or realistic datasets and learn data provenance, consent, quality, representation, imbalance, and limitations.
- **Python**: Python becomes a genuine working tool. Students should read, modify, debug, and progressively write code for data and simple AI/ML tasks.
- **Libraries**: teacher-guided use of age-appropriate Python data/ML libraries is allowed, but students should understand what the library call is accomplishing conceptually.
- **Generative AI**: move beyond chatbot usage. Students should understand context, limitations, evaluation, retrieval, tools, and system-level applications.
- **LLMs**: explain tokens, context, probabilistic generation, embeddings, and transformer concepts without requiring matrix-heavy architecture.
- **RAG**: students should understand why external trusted information can be retrieved and supplied to a language model. By Class 10, a guided small RAG demonstration/project is appropriate where resources permit.
- **Agents**: teach agents as goal-directed software systems with limited permissions, tools, state, and human oversight — not as digital humans.
- **APIs**: introduce APIs as controlled ways for software systems to exchange requests and responses. Students may perform simple teacher-supervised API exercises without handling production secrets.
- **Evaluation**: students must justify claims about model quality with evidence.
- **Deployment**: introduce the difference between a notebook experiment and a usable AI system, including interface, reliability, monitoring, privacy, cost, and safety.
- **Responsible AI**: integrate privacy, fairness, security, transparency, accountability, accessibility, sustainability, and human oversight into technical projects.
- **Human-centred principle**: technical performance alone does not determine whether an AI system should be deployed.
- **Bharat context**: projects should regularly consider India's languages, accessibility needs, rural/urban diversity, infrastructure constraints, agriculture, healthcare, education, MSMEs, public services, and community challenges.
- **Tool independence**: concepts must remain useful even when specific commercial AI products change.
- **AI-assisted coding**: students may use approved AI coding assistants, but they must understand, test, debug, and explain submitted code.
- **Assessment**: combine written conceptual work, coding, model evaluation, practical demonstrations, case analysis, documentation, presentations, and projects.
- **Evidence and citation**: AI-generated factual material used in assignments should be checked against reliable sources.
- **Failure analysis**: students should be rewarded for identifying where their model fails and proposing improvements rather than hiding poor results.

## Model evaluation examples

Students should understand that the "best model" depends on the problem.

Example:

A medical screening system that identifies 98 out of 100 people correctly may still be unsafe if the two mistakes are seriously ill patients incorrectly classified as healthy.

A spam filter may tolerate some errors differently from a medical screening system.

Students should therefore ask:

- What kinds of mistakes does the model make?
- Who is affected by those mistakes?
- How serious are false positives?
- How serious are false negatives?
- Is the dataset representative?
- Does the model work equally well for relevant groups?
- Is human review required?

Do not teach model evaluation as simply "higher accuracy = better AI."

## Generative AI and LLM learning strand

Students should progress through:

Generative AI
→ Language models
→ Tokens and context
→ Prompting
→ Output evaluation
→ Hallucination
→ Embeddings
→ Semantic search
→ RAG
→ Tools
→ Agents.

Do not jump directly from "ChatGPT-like chatbot" to "AI agent" without teaching the concepts connecting them.

Students should understand that:

- LLMs generate outputs from learned patterns and context.
- Fluent output is not proof of truth.
- A model's internal knowledge can be incomplete or outdated.
- Retrieval can provide relevant external information.
- Tools can allow an AI system to perform specific operations.
- Agents combine goals, reasoning/planning processes, state, and tool use.
- Greater capability requires stronger controls and human oversight.

## AI cybersecurity strand

Introduce security through practical, age-appropriate scenarios.

Topics may include:

- Sensitive information accidentally entered into prompts
- Malicious or misleading prompts
- Prompt injection
- Unsafe external content
- AI-generated phishing
- Deepfake impersonation
- Generated malicious links/content
- Excessive permissions given to AI agents
- API-key protection
- Why secrets must not be hard-coded into public applications

Students should learn:

AI security is not only about protecting the AI model.

It is also about protecting:

Users → Data → Credentials → Tools → Connected systems → Outputs.

Do not provide offensive cyberattack training.

Suitable projects may include:

## Assessment approach

Assessment should measure four dimensions:

### 1. Knowledge

Can the student explain the relevant AI concepts?

### 2. Application

Can the student actually use the concepts to build/test something?

### 3. Evaluation

Can the student measure performance, identify errors, verify outputs, and question limitations?

### 4. Responsible Creation

Can the student design an AI solution that considers people, privacy, fairness, security, accessibility, sustainability, and human oversight?

Project marks should not be awarded mainly for attractive UI or impressive AI-generated demonstrations.

## Avoid

- University-level calculus or linear algebra.
- Detailed backpropagation derivations.
- Transformer mathematics.
- Training large language models.
- Expensive GPU-dependent projects as essential coursework.
- Copy-pasting complete AI-generated projects.
- Treating prompting alone as AI engineering.
- Treating API calls alone as proof of understanding AI.
- Using accuracy as the only evaluation measure.
- Deploying student-built high-stakes AI systems as real decision makers.
- Uploading identifiable student, medical, biometric, financial, or other sensitive data to public AI services.
- Hard-coding API secrets in student projects.
- Unsupervised autonomous agents with consequential permissions.
- Presenting AI-generated citations as trustworthy without verification.
- Teaching one commercial AI product as if it represents the entire field.
- Treating AI as the solution to every problem.

## Calibration for this band
- **Language**: full technical vocabulary, used the way it would be in an intro course — precision, recall, regression, clustering, etc. — always still defined, but no longer over-simplified.
- **Depth**: an end-to-end applied view — students should be able to walk through the AI project cycle for a real problem, not just define terms.
- **Examples**: applied domains relevant to India — agriculture (crop disease detection), healthcare access, accessibility tools, local business use cases, governance/public-service applications.
- **Lesson length**: 40–60 min or double periods for project work.
- **Activities**: guided data exploration on a real (school-safe) dataset, building and evaluating a simple classifier, prompt-engineering exercises with a generative AI tool, structured debates on bias/misinformation case studies.
- **Math**: real but bounded — percentages, ratios, basic probability, reading and computing a confusion matrix, precision/recall by hand on a small example. No calculus, no matrix algebra required (can be mentioned conceptually for neural networks).
- **Coding**: Python-based projects become standard (e.g., using pandas/scikit-learn-level tools or a simplified educational wrapper); no-code/block-based tools remain valid alternatives where devices/Python aren't available.
- **Projects**: multi-session applied projects addressing a real problem in one of the listed domains — full AI project cycle, with a written report and presentation/viva.
- **Assessment**: scenario-based and applied questions, practical coding/no-code tasks, case-study analysis, project rubric assessment, viva questions probing reasoning (not just output).
- **Responsible AI**: case studies, fact-checking exercises, structured ethical-analysis frameworks (who is affected, what could go wrong, how would you check), documentation of AI use in projects.

## Sample analogies / framings that work at this level
- The AI project cycle mirrors the scientific method: define the question, gather evidence (data), form a model, test it, refine it.
- Precision vs. recall: precision is "of the things I flagged, how many were actually right"; recall is "of the things that were actually right, how many did I catch" — use a real scenario like a spam filter or medical screening to ground it.
- A generative AI model is a very sophisticated pattern-completion system trained on huge amounts of text/images — it predicts what's plausible, not what's true.

Avoid: assuming prior programming fluency without checking; unqualified claims about AI "intelligence" or consciousness; skipping the evaluation/limitations step of any project.
