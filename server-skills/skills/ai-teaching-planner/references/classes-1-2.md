# Classes 1–2: Discovering Smart Machines

ELI level: Class 1 = ELI6, Class 2 = ELI7. Ages ~6–8.

## Topic list (sequence roughly in this order)

1. Machines around us (fans, toasters, washing machines, calculators, traffic lights)
2. Humans and machines — what each is good at
3. What makes a machine "seem smart"?
4. Giving clear step-by-step instructions (the seed of "algorithm"; do not require the technical word at this age)
5. Patterns, matching, sorting, colours, sounds, shapes, and simple sequences
6. How machines notice the world — seeing, hearing, and sensing through simple examples such as cameras, microphones, and sensors
7. Introduction to robots — robots are machines, but not every robot is smart and not every smart machine is a robot
8. Simple everyday examples of AI (voice assistants, face unlock, photo search, recommendations, smart translation)
9. Machines can learn from examples — introduce only through games and demonstrations, without technical explanation
10. Machines can make mistakes — compare a machine's answer with what a child or teacher knows
11. Humans stay in charge — smart machines are helpers, not people, and important decisions should be checked by people
12. Safe and responsible use of smart technology (ask a trusted grown-up, do not share personal information, do not blindly believe everything a smart machine says)
13. AI around us in Bharat — simple, relatable examples such as Indian-language voice/translation tools, farming helpers, accessibility tools, and other technology that helps people

## Calibration for this band

- **Language**: very short sentences and everyday words only. Avoid technical jargon. Terms such as "algorithm," "model," "training data," and "machine learning" are not required. "Artificial Intelligence (AI)" may be introduced gently, especially in Class 2, after children first understand the idea of a "smart helper machine."
- **Depth**: one main idea per lesson, reinforced through repetition, stories, play, observation, and conversation. Do not stack multiple technical concepts into one lesson.
- **Examples**: use things from the child's own home, school, neighbourhood, and Bharat — fan, calculator, TV remote, traffic light, phone camera, voice assistant, toy robot, automatic door, language/voice tool.
- **Human-centred principle**: repeatedly reinforce that people make choices and machines are tools that help people. Never imply that AI "thinks," "knows," "feels," or understands exactly like a human.
- **Lesson length**: short attention spans — favour 30–40 minute lessons with activity switches approximately every 5–8 minutes.
- **Activities**: stories, games, pictures, drawing, sorting objects, role-play ("be the robot"), sound/shape matching, physical movement, teacher demonstrations, and simple observation activities. Screens are not required for most lessons.
- **Perception activities**: include simple games about how people and machines notice things — eyes/camera, ears/microphone, touch/sensor — without teaching technical sensor theory.
- **Learning-from-examples activities**: use child-safe demonstrations such as showing several examples of fruits, animals, colours, shapes, or classroom objects and asking how a "smart helper" might learn to group or recognise similar things. Do not introduce mathematical machine-learning concepts.
- **Math**: none beyond age-appropriate counting, comparing, grouping, matching, and sorting (for example, how many red blocks vs blue blocks).
- **Coding**: not compulsory. If used at all, keep it unplugged: physical direction cards, sequencing steps, "if this happens, then do this" games, maze movement, and "be the robot." No programming syntax is required.
- **Projects**: single-session craft, drawing, sorting, storytelling, observation, or role-play activities. Do not require multi-day technical projects.
- **Assessment**: oral questions, thumbs up/down, matching pictures, arranging steps, drawing, teacher observation, simple show-and-tell, and role-play. No formal written AI tests.
- **Responsible AI**: teach through simple rules and stories: "ask a trusted grown-up," "do not tell a smart machine private information," "machines can make mistakes," "check important answers with a person," and "use technology kindly."
- **Bharat context**: occasionally use age-appropriate examples showing technology helping people speak different Indian languages, farmers, teachers, doctors, people with disabilities, families, and communities. Keep this experiential rather than teaching organisations, schemes, policies, or technical platforms by name.
- **Inclusivity**: avoid assuming every child owns a smartphone, computer, smart speaker, or has internet access. Every essential concept must also be teachable through unplugged classroom activities.
- **Teacher role**: the teacher should guide, question, demonstrate, and encourage curiosity. AI or digital tools must never replace teacher judgement at this age.

## Expected learning outcomes by the end of Class 2

A child should be able to:

1. Identify several machines used in everyday life.
2. Explain in simple words that humans and machines can be good at different things.
3. Follow and give a short sequence of clear instructions.
4. Recognise simple patterns and group similar objects.
5. Explain that some machines can use cameras, microphones, or other ways to notice things around them.
6. Recognise a few everyday examples of smart/AI-enabled technology.
7. Understand, at a very simple level, that a smart machine can use examples to become better at recognising something.
8. Understand that smart machines can sometimes give wrong answers.
9. Know that people should check important decisions and remain responsible.
10. Follow basic safety rules when using smart technology.
11. Recognise that AI and smart technology can be used to help people and communities in Bharat.

## Sample analogies that work at this level

- A smart machine is like a very obedient helper: it needs clear instructions and can still make mistakes.
- Giving directions to a robot is like telling a friend how to reach your desk one small step at a time.
- Sorting toys by colour or shape helps children understand how things can be grouped by patterns.
- A camera can be described as one way a machine can "look," but explain that a camera does not see and understand the world exactly as a person does.
- A microphone helps a machine receive sounds, just as our ears help us hear, but the machine may misunderstand what was said.
- Showing a smart helper several examples is like showing a child several pictures of mangoes before asking them to find another mango.
- A smart machine giving an answer is like a helper making a suggestion: sometimes the suggestion is useful and sometimes a person needs to correct it.

## Avoid

- Probability, statistics, datasets, neural networks, model architecture, formal machine learning, or other advanced technical concepts.
- Technical definitions that children are expected to memorise.
- Saying or implying that AI is a person, has feelings, is always correct, or understands the world exactly as humans do.
- Long lectures.
- Screens-only lessons.
- Making reading or writing fluency a bottleneck for understanding AI.
- Asking children to create personal accounts on AI services.
- Asking children to enter their name, address, photographs, phone number, school details, family information, or other personal information into public AI tools.
- Unsupervised chatbot use.
- Brand-heavy teaching in which children learn particular commercial AI products instead of transferable ideas.
- Fear-based teaching about AI.
- Formal written examinations as the main assessment method.

## Calibration for this band
- **Language**: very short sentences, everyday words only, no jargon at all (not even simplified jargon — skip terms like "algorithm," "data," "AI" itself can be introduced late and softly, e.g. "smart helper machines").
- **Depth**: one idea per lesson, reinforced through repetition and play, not stacked concepts.
- **Examples**: things in the child's own home and classroom — fan, calculator, TV remote, a toy that lights up.
- **Lesson length**: short attention spans — favor 30–40 minute lessons with frequent activity switches (every 5–8 minutes).
- **Activities**: stories, games, pictures, drawing, sorting objects, role-play ("be the robot"), sound/shape matching, physical movement. No screens required.
- **Math**: none, or only counting/sorting (how many red blocks vs blue blocks).
- **Coding**: not compulsory. If used at all, only unplugged (physical direction cards, "if-then" as a simple game) — no actual programming interface.
- **Projects**: a single-session craft or role-play activity, not a multi-day project.
- **Assessment**: oral questions, thumbs up/down, matching pictures, simple show-and-tell — no written tests.
- **Responsible AI**: framed as simple safety rules and stories ("always ask a grown-up before talking to a machine that talks back," "machines can make mistakes too").

## Sample analogies that work at this level
- A smart machine is like a very obedient but literal-minded helper — it only does exactly what it's told.
- Sorting toys by color/shape is what a machine does with a "pattern."
- A robot vacuum "sees" the room the way a child with their eyes closed uses their hands to find the wall.

Avoid: probability, data privacy debates, technical vocabulary, screens-only lessons, anything requiring reading fluency as a bottleneck.
