# Classes 3–5: Foundations of AI Thinking

ELI level: Class 3 = ELI8, Class 4 = ELI9, Class 5 = ELI10. Ages ~8–11.

## Topic list (sequence roughly in this order)

1. What is Artificial Intelligence? — a simple working definition and how AI differs from an ordinary machine
2. Humans, machines, robots, automation, and AI — similarities and differences
3. Algorithms as clear step-by-step instructions
4. Inputs, instructions, and outputs — what goes into a system and what comes out
5. Data as information — pictures, words, sounds, numbers, choices, and observations
6. Collecting, grouping, sorting, and labelling simple data
7. Patterns and classification — how people and machines group similar things
8. How machines sense the world — cameras, microphones, and simple sensors
9. How AI learns from examples — teach a simple classifier using examples rather than rules
10. Training examples and testing examples — introduce the idea through activities, without formal machine-learning terminology
11. Image recognition — how AI can identify or classify pictures
12. Speech and sound recognition — how machines can work with spoken words and sounds
13. Language, voice assistants, and translation
14. Chatbots and conversational AI — what they can and cannot understand
15. Recommendation systems — why apps suggest videos, songs, products, or other content
16. Generative AI — machines that can create text, pictures, sounds, or other content from instructions
17. Giving good instructions to AI — clear questions, useful context, and checking the result
18. AI can make mistakes — wrong answers, misclassification, and simple examples of AI hallucination without requiring the technical term
19. Why examples matter — incomplete or unfair examples can produce poor or unfair results
20. Humans stay responsible — when AI should help and when a person should decide
21. Privacy and personal information — what should never be casually shared with an AI system
22. Fairness and inclusion — AI should work fairly for different people, languages, abilities, and communities
23. Real or AI-made? — age-appropriate introduction to AI-generated images/text and why we should verify what we see
24. AI for good — examples in education, healthcare, accessibility, agriculture, environment, and community services
25. AI in Bharat — Indian-language AI, voice and translation technologies, accessibility, agriculture, education, and other India-relevant examples
26. AI and Bharatiya languages — explore how technology can hear, read, translate, and speak different Indian languages
27. Problem solving with AI — identify a small real-life problem and decide whether AI is actually useful for solving it
28. Block-based / no-code AI activities — train, test, and improve simple age-appropriate AI models
29. Human + AI teamwork — use AI as a helper while the student remains the thinker, checker, and creator
30. Mini AI project — create or demonstrate a simple AI-assisted solution to a school, home, accessibility, language, environment, or community problem

For a first lesson:
- Class 3 should normally begin around topics 1–3.

## Progression within this band

### Class 3 — Discover and Understand

Primary goal: recognise AI, understand instructions/data/patterns, and distinguish AI from ordinary machines.

Emphasise:
- Topics 1–10
- Everyday AI
- Algorithms
- Input/output
- Data
- Sorting/classification
- Machine perception
- Learning from examples
- Safety and human responsibility

Students should primarily explore, classify, discuss, role-play, and experiment.

### Class 4 — Explore and Apply

Primary goal: understand how common AI applications use examples, patterns, language, images, and sounds.

Emphasise:
- Topics 6–20
- Classification
- Training/testing through examples
- Image recognition
- Speech recognition
- Translation
- Chatbots
- Recommendations
- Generative AI introduction
- Checking AI output
- Human oversight

Students should increasingly use guided no-code AI activities rather than only observing AI.

### Class 5 — Create and Question

Primary goal: begin creating simple AI solutions while developing critical judgement.

Emphasise:
- Topics 16–30
- Generative AI
- Clear prompting/instructions
- AI mistakes
- Fairness
- Privacy
- AI-generated content
- Bharat-focused applications
- Problem scoping
- No-code model creation
- Human + AI collaboration
- Mini-projects

By the end of Class 5, students should move from "What is AI?" toward "How might we use AI responsibly to help solve this problem?"

## Calibration for this band

- **Language**: simple but accurate vocabulary. Introduce and repeatedly use terms such as "Artificial Intelligence (AI)," "algorithm," "data," "input," "output," "pattern," "classification," and "training examples" when developmentally appropriate. Explain every new term through examples before expecting students to use it.
- **Depth**: explain the idea behind a system without mathematical or engineering detail. Students should understand what goes in, what the system does conceptually, what comes out, and why the result may sometimes be wrong.
- **Examples**: school, home, neighbourhood, transport, agriculture, languages, accessibility, games, photographs, maps, voice assistants, recommendations, and other experiences children can recognise.
- **Learning progression**: move gradually from Understand → Apply → Create. Do not introduce advanced terminology simply because a modern AI tool uses it.
- **Human-centred principle**: AI is a tool created by people. People decide where and how it should be used and remain responsible for important decisions.
- **Lesson length**: typically 35–45 minutes with explanation broken up by questions, demonstrations, games, discussion, or hands-on activity.
- **Activities**: sorting/classification games, role-play, drawing decision processes, collecting small classroom datasets, image/sound classification demonstrations, chatbot comparison, AI-output checking, no-code model training, and problem-solving activities.
- **Data activities**: use small, child-safe datasets such as classroom objects, leaves, fruits, shapes, animals, sounds, or teacher-provided images. Avoid collecting unnecessary personal information.
- **AI learning activities**: where tools permit, allow students to provide labelled examples, train a simple model, test it with new examples, notice mistakes, add or improve examples, and test again.
- **Generative AI**: use only teacher-approved, age-appropriate environments. Students should learn that generated content may sound confident while still being incorrect.
- **Prompting**: teach prompting as "giving clear instructions to an AI helper," not as advanced prompt engineering. Encourage students to state the task clearly, add useful information, examine the answer, and improve the instruction when necessary.
- **Math**: age-appropriate counting, simple tables, comparisons, frequencies, bar charts, and basic ideas such as "more examples" or "fewer correct answers." No probability formulas or ML mathematics.
- **Coding**: block-based or no-code activities are preferred. Programming syntax is not required for mastering this band.
- **Projects**: short individual or group projects lasting one lesson to a few lessons. Every project should have a clearly stated problem, users/people affected, proposed solution, test, and reflection.
- **Assessment**: oral explanation, diagrams, sorting/classification tasks, short worksheets, demonstrations, project rubrics, reflection, and "explain why" questions. Avoid assessment based mainly on memorising definitions.
- **Responsible AI**: integrate safety, privacy, fairness, verification, human responsibility, and respectful use throughout the curriculum rather than teaching ethics only once.
- **Bharat context**: regularly include examples relevant to India's languages, communities, agriculture, accessibility, education, healthcare, transport, environment, and local problem-solving.
- **Inclusivity**: examples should represent different languages, abilities, genders, communities, and social contexts. Students should begin noticing that an AI system may work better for some groups than others.
- **Teacher role**: teachers guide questioning and verification. AI output must not automatically be treated as the correct answer.
- **Tool independence**: teach transferable AI concepts rather than making the curriculum dependent on one commercial chatbot, company, or product.
- **Screen balance**: meaningful AI activities may use computers/tablets, but every major concept should have an unplugged or teacher-led alternative where practical.

## Expected learning outcomes by the end of Class 5

A student should be able to:

1. Explain Artificial Intelligence in simple, age-appropriate language.
2. Distinguish, at a basic level, between an ordinary machine, automation, robot, and AI-enabled system.
3. Create and follow a simple algorithm as a sequence of instructions.
4. Identify the input and output of a simple system.
5. Explain what data means using everyday examples.
6. Collect, sort, label, and represent a small child-safe dataset.
7. Explain how patterns can help classify objects or information.
8. Explain that some AI systems learn patterns from examples rather than receiving every possible rule explicitly.
9. Train and test a simple no-code classifier where suitable tools are available.
10. Explain why testing with new examples is important.
11. Recognise everyday uses of image recognition, speech recognition, translation, chatbots, recommendation systems, and generative AI.
12. Give clear instructions to an AI tool and improve an instruction after examining the result.
13. Recognise that AI can make mistakes and that confident-looking output is not necessarily correct.
14. Check important AI-generated information against a teacher, trusted source, observation, or other evidence.
15. Identify personal information that should not be shared unnecessarily with AI services.
16. Explain in simple terms how incomplete or unbalanced examples may make an AI system work poorly for some people.
17. Explain why humans remain responsible for important decisions involving AI.
18. Identify examples of AI being used to help people and communities in Bharat.
19. Describe how AI can support different Bharatiya languages and accessibility needs.
20. Decide, for a simple problem, whether AI is useful or whether a simpler non-AI solution may be better.
21. Design or demonstrate a simple AI/no-code project addressing a school, home, environmental, accessibility, language, or community need.
22. Explain what their project does, what information it uses, where it may fail, and how a person should remain involved.

## Sample analogies that work at this level

- An algorithm is like a recipe: the steps and their order matter.
- Data is like a collection of clues that can help us notice patterns.
- Training examples are like practice questions: they help the system learn what kinds of patterns to look for.
- Testing is like giving the system a new question it did not practise before and seeing whether it can use what it learned.
- Classification is like organising books into different shelves based on what they are about.
- Image recognition is like playing a picture-identification game, except the computer looks for patterns it learned from examples.
- Speech recognition turns patterns in sounds into words, but accents and background noise can sometimes confuse it.
- A recommendation system is like a helper making suggestions from patterns in previous choices, but its suggestions are not commands.
- A chatbot is like a very fast language helper that can produce convincing sentences but may still misunderstand or invent information.
- Giving an AI a prompt is like giving instructions to a helper: clearer instructions usually make the task easier to understand.
- An unfair or incomplete set of examples is like practising only one type of question and then being tested on many different types.
- Human + AI teamwork is like using a calculator: the tool can help, but the person still needs to decide what problem to solve and whether the answer makes sense.

## Bharat-focused examples

Use Bharat-focused examples naturally throughout lessons rather than isolating them into one memorisation chapter.

Suitable examples include:

- A voice system understanding different Indian languages.
- Translating a classroom message from one Bharatiya language into another.
- AI helping a farmer recognise a possible crop problem from a photograph.
- AI helping a person who cannot hear by turning speech into text.
- AI helping a person who cannot speak by turning typed text into speech.
- Technology helping teachers create learning materials in different languages.
- AI assisting doctors while the doctor remains responsible for medical decisions.
- Smart traffic or transport applications.
- Identifying waste for recycling.
- Helping communities understand weather or environmental information.

At this age, focus on the problem being solved and the people being helped. Do not require memorisation of government programmes, AI-company names, model names, or technical infrastructure.

## Safety rules for student AI use

Students should learn:

1. Never enter private information simply because an AI asks for it.
2. Do not upload another person's photograph, voice, schoolwork, or information without appropriate permission.
3. AI answers can be wrong.
4. Check important information.
5. Tell a teacher or trusted adult if AI produces frightening, inappropriate, confusing, or suspicious content.
6. Do not use AI to bully, deceive, impersonate, or embarrass someone.
7. AI-generated pictures, voices, and text may look real even when they are not.
8. Give credit when AI meaningfully helps create schoolwork, according to the teacher's rules.
9. Do your own thinking; do not use AI simply to avoid learning.
10. A human remains responsible for how AI is used.

## Avoid

- Formal machine-learning mathematics.
- Neural-network architecture.
- Regression, clustering algorithms, gradient descent, or other secondary/senior-level ML topics.
- Detailed LLM or transformer architecture.
- API programming.
- Complex prompt engineering terminology.
- Treating chatbot use as equivalent to learning AI.
- Memorising brand names or commercial AI products.
- Allowing AI to complete assignments without student thinking or reflection.
- Collecting biometric or sensitive student data for classroom AI demonstrations.
- Publicly uploading children's faces or voices merely to demonstrate AI.
- Unsupervised use of public generative-AI services.
- Presenting AI-generated output as automatically factual.
- Teaching that AI "thinks," "feels," "knows," or understands exactly like a person.
- Presenting AI as the solution to every problem.
- Requiring access to expensive devices or paid AI tools to achieve essential learning outcomes.

## Calibration for this band
- **Language**: simple but real vocabulary; introduce "algorithm," "data," "pattern," "AI" as proper terms with plain-English definitions given before first use.
- **Depth**: one core concept per lesson, but concepts can now build on each other across lessons (e.g., patterns → classification → recommendation systems).
- **Examples**: school and home routines, familiar apps (a music or video app suggesting something), simple games.
- **Lesson length**: 40 minutes typical; can sustain a single focus for ~15–20 minutes before switching activity type.
- **Activities**: sorting/classification games, "be the algorithm" role-play, simple flowcharts drawn by hand, guided use of a voice assistant, block-based tools (e.g., Scratch-style) if devices are available.
- **Math**: basic — counting, simple grouping/categorization, very basic charts (bar graphs of sorted items). No formulas.
- **Coding**: optional and block-based/no-code only; never text-based programming yet.
- **Projects**: single or two-session projects — e.g., build a simple sorting game, create a flowchart for a daily routine.
- **Assessment**: MCQ, true/false, fill-in-the-blank, short answer, simple scenario questions ("Is this AI or not? Why?"), plus oral/practical checks.
- **Responsible AI**: simple rules with reasons — "AI can be wrong, so we check," "don't tell a chatbot your address," introduced through short stories or scenarios, not abstract debate.

## Sample analogies that work at this level
- An algorithm is like a recipe — exact steps in exact order.
- A recommendation system is like a friend who remembers what you liked before and guesses what you'll like next (and sometimes guesses wrong).
- Classification is like sorting laundry — the machine looks for a feature (color, size) and puts things in matching piles.

Avoid: neural network internals, statistics/probability formulas, unsupervised text-based coding, open-ended ethical debate without concrete examples.
